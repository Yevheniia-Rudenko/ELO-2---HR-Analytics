# HR Employee Attrition - Data Exploration- What the Data Tells Us

## The Big Picture

We've been digging into our employee data to understand who's leaving and why. Looking at 1,470 employee records, we found some clear patterns that can help us tackle our turnover problem.

### **Strong Predictors of Attrition**

**Top factors that significantly increase attrition risk:**

| Factor | Impact on Attrition |
|--------|---------------------|
| **Working Overtime** | 3× higher attrition (30% vs 10% for non-overtime workers) |
| **Frequent Business Travel** | Significantly higher attrition than rarely-traveling employees |
| **Young Age (18-25)** | ~35% attrition rate - highest of all age groups |
| **Short Tenure** | Employees with fewer years at company leave more often |
| **Sales Department** | Highest attrition among departments |
| **Sales Representative Role** | Highest attrition among job roles |

## The Turnover Numbers

- Out of everyone on our team, about 16% decided to leave - that's 237 people
- The vast majority (84%) are sticking with us, which is good
- But that 16% represents real talent walking out the door, and we need to understand why

## What Really Drives People Away

### The Overtime Problem

This one's clear as day - people working overtime are **three times more likely to leave** than those who don't. Think about that: if you're regularly putting in extra hours, you're three times more likely to be heading for the exit. That's not just a trend - that's a flashing red light.

### Travel Takes Its Toll

Employees who travel frequently for business show much higher turnover. Constantly being on the road seems to be wearing people down. The "rarely travel" group sticks around much longer.

### The Youth Exodus

Our youngest employees (18-25 year olds) are leaving at an alarming rate - about 35% of them. That's more than one in three. Meanwhile, our more experienced team members are much more likely to stay. We're losing fresh talent and institutional knowledge isn't walking out the door.

### New Hires Need More Support

Employees who've been here less than three years are at higher risk. The "honeymoon period" seems to end quickly for some, and if we don't give them reasons to stay, they find them elsewhere.

## Department and Role Hotspots

The Sales department is seeing the highest turnover, especially among Sales Representatives. Whatever's happening there needs attention. Research & Development and HR have better retention rates, so maybe there are lessons to learn from those teams.

## The Good News

Most of our people report decent job satisfaction and reasonable work-life balance. Average commute isn't terrible at about 9 miles. People generally like their work environment. So we've got a good foundation - we're not dealing with widespread unhappiness.

## What This Means for Us

### Immediate Actions

1. **Look at overtime policies** - Is this mandatory or cultural? Can we redistribute workload?
2. **Review travel schedules** - Are we burning people out with constant travel?
3. **Create a "first 3 years" program** - Better onboarding, mentorship, and check-ins for new team members
4. **Engage our younger employees** - Career path conversations, development opportunities, meaningful work

### Longer-Term Thinking

We need to understand if people are leaving for:

- More money elsewhere
- Better work-life balance
- Career advancement we're not providing
- Management or culture issues

## The Data Quality

Good news here - our data is clean. No missing information, no duplicates. We can trust what we're seeing.

## Bottom Line

We're not dealing with a company-wide crisis, but we have specific, fixable problems. The patterns are clear enough that we can take targeted action rather than guessing. Let's start with the overtime and young employee issues - those seem to be our biggest pressure points.

The goal isn't just to reduce a number. It's about keeping good people who want to be here. This data gives us a roadmap for doing exactly that.
